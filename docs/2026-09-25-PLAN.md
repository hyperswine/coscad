# Build plans: `coscad plan`

`coscad plan foo.assemble` turns an assembly into a numbered build sequence
a person can follow without backtracking: nuts go in before the slot they
need is consumed, every screw is driven with its driver clear, each step
names which face rests on the bench, and every fastener carries a torque
for its host's material. The design rationale is in the assembly
instruction generator design note; this file is the working reference.

## What you write

Placement can come from an `asm` expression or from `fixed` / `mate`
constraints. The planner uses the same solved poses as the assembly loader
and checker. Beyond placement, it needs a few part hints and one `fastener`
line per screw group:

```
rx ← rail200.coscad ×0 profile=2020 material=aluminium
rz ← rail160.coscad ×0 profile=2020 material=printed ends=open,blocked
br ← flat90.coscad ×16

fastener M5x10 br#1 rx#1 bot at=30            // spec clamped host face [options]
fastener M5x10 px  ry#2 rt ×2                 // two screws, spaced along the overlap
fastener M5x10 br1 railA top through=6        // counterbored bracket: 6 mm under the head
plan beam=30 rest=bot,top,fwd,bak             // optional planner settings
```

- `profile=2020` marks a rail: all four long faces are T-slots, both ends
  open unless `ends=open,blocked` (min end, max end along the rail).
- `material=aluminium|printed|steel` sets the torque class; `aluminum` is
  also accepted. `pla`, `petg`, `abs`, `asa`, `tpu`, `nylon`, and `pc` use
  the printed class, including premade `×0` parts. Values are case insensitive.
  Parts printed
  by this assembly (`×n`, n > 0) default to `printed`, premade (`×0`)
  parts to `aluminium`. `torque=` on a part or a fastener overrides.
  `mass=` (grams) overrides the volume-based estimate. Mass and torque
  overrides must be finite and greater than zero.
- `after=instance[,instance...]` on a part declares placement prerequisites
  that the box model cannot discover. For example,
  `axle2 ← axle.coscad ×1 after=rotor#1` keeps a rotor's entry open until
  it is installed. Use numbered names for repeated instances. Unknown,
  ambiguous and cyclic references are errors.
- `fastener spec clamped host face`: `spec` is `M<d>x<len>`; `clamped` and
  `host` are instance names (`br#3` when a part is placed more than once,
  the bare name when once); `face` is the host face the screw enters
  (`top bot lft rt fwd bak`, world frame). Options: `×n` screws spaced
  along the overlap, `at=mm[,mm]` positions from the host's marked end,
  `nut=dropin|slidein|hex|none` (`drop-in`, `slide-in`, and `captive` for
  `hex` are also accepted), `head=button|socket`, `through=mm` (material
  under the head when the clamped part is thinner at the hole than its
  bounding box, e.g. counterbored), `torque=Nm`.
- `nut=hex pocket=face`: the host is not a rail; the screw meets a plain
  hex nut captive in a pocket that opens on that host face. Nuts still go
  in before the part that covers them, the pocket face may not be on the
  bench or under another part while they go in, and the plan says
  "hex nut into the pocket on the top face" instead of slot positions.
  `examples/assemble/ball/` (two hemisphere shells bolted to a core disc)
  is the worked example.
- `pocket=clamped.face`: the nut pocket is in the clamped part, not the
  host (a corner block whose screw crosses it into a bar end and finds
  its nut on the block's far face). The nut goes in with the part in
  hand, so nothing can cover it; if that face points down when the screw
  is driven the step says to hold the nut in.
- `nut=none`: the screw threads into the host itself (a printed bar, a
  tapped block); nothing to preload, the reach check still applies.
- `fastener peg clamped host face`: a friction joint (a peg in a hole,
  a bar in a socket) with no screw: no driver, no torque, no nut, but
  the same ordering and support rules, and "Push ... until it seats" in
  the steps. `examples/assemble/tesseract/` (a wire-frame cube whose
  inner cube hangs on eight diagonal pegs) uses both.
- `inset=mm` on a peg joint moves its seating plane inward from the named
  host face, for an internal shoulder or recessed socket. For example,
  `fastener peg rotor#1 axle1 top inset=13` seats the rotor on a shoulder
  13 mm below the axle's top. The inset must be within the host and is not
  supported for screws. This describes the joint seat, not a checked
  collision-free insertion path.
- `plan` options: `beam=N` (search width; 30 default, wider is slower
  and better), `rest=faces` to restrict allowed rest faces, cost weights
  `flip vertical driver shrink sibling loose`, `astar=N` to try exact
  search with an expansion cap first.
  `beam` and `astar` must be positive whole numbers; cost weights must be
  finite and non-negative. Unknown plan options and invalid rest faces are
  errors, and each option may appear only once across all `plan` lines.

Part names must be unique. Repeated options on a part or fastener are errors,
including repeated `×count` or `▽orientation`. Unknown fastener options are
errors; unknown part hints such as `seam=rear` remain available as metadata.
Recognized hints are checked before planning: `profile` must be `2020`, and
`ends` must contain exactly two `open` or `blocked` values.

Distances in `at=`, `through=`, and `inset=` must be finite and non-negative.
Screw diameter and length must be positive; write `peg` for a friction joint.
Fastener quantities are from 1 to 10,000. Part print quantities are from 0 to
10,000, with a total expanded print quantity of at most 10,000 across recursive
assemblies. Expanded fasteners also total at most 10,000, including explicit
`at=` positions. These are input limits, not a promise that planning a large
assembly will be fast.

`plate width depth [margin]` requires finite positive dimensions and a finite,
non-negative margin that leaves usable space on both axes; the default margin
is 6 mm. `clearance mm` requires a finite, non-negative distance.

Accessibility is judged on boxes: a driver is blocked only when its
cylinder really enters another part's *oriented* box (the part's own box
under its placement, so a diagonal strut is a thin rod, not the block its
axis-aligned box would be), and touching does not count, so a shell whose
box brushes the neighbouring shell's box, as in the ball, does not block
the screw on the other side. A part may be placed "held" against anything
already placed that it will be joined to, on either side of the joint. A
slender part (three times longer than wide) stood on end costs the same
as a rail stood on end and gets the same warning.

The planner derives the driver axis (the host face's outward normal), the
screw positions, the thread reach (length minus material under the head),
and the T-nut preload sheet. Along the host, positions use the overlap or
explicit `at=` distances. Across a `profile=2020` side face, they stay on
the host's slot centre even when an inset panel covers only part of the
face. A clamped part that does not cover that centre is rejected. Other
hosts and rail end faces use the transverse overlap centre.

The [200 mm cube](../examples/assemble/plan/cube.assemble) is a worked
constraint-based assembly: both holes of each of its 16 brackets are mated
to rail interfaces, and its six panel bores match explicit screw distances.
See the [geometry and hardware verification](2026-10-12-CUBE-CONSTRAINTS.md).

## Frame-based screws and bearing seats

Use a named host frame instead of a world face when the joint has an
explicit bore. Each line describes one physical screw:

```coscad
fastener M4x12 arm center @root_hole seat=@head_seat head=socket nut=hex pocket=@nut_seat
```

The host frame origin is the hole's interface point; its outward +Z axis
points toward the screw head. `seat=@head_seat` selects a frame on the
clamped part at the underside of the head, including any counterbore.
Its normal points in the same direction. `pocket=@nut_seat` selects the
nut's bearing seat on the host, with its normal pointing away from the
head. `pocket=clamped.@nut_seat` selects a seat on the clamped part instead.
These are bearing surfaces, not the centres of the screw head or nut.

The solved part poses carry every point and direction into world coordinates.
The planner checks that the frames are coaxial, their normals agree, and
seats lie within their parts' bounds. Screw reach is the screw length minus
the distance between the head and nut bearing seats. For a T-nut or threaded
host, it measures from the head seat to the host interface. Existing
minimum-reach checks still apply. Frame-based rail holes must lie on the
2020 slot centre and outer face. `at=`, `×count`, `through=` and `inset=`
cannot be combined with a host frame; they would provide a second source
of placement or seating information. Pegs retain their face/inset syntax.

`preload=hand` on a part prepares its nuts while it is independent, before
placing that part. This is useful for opposing rail slots or underside
pockets: turn the loose part to reach them and retain the nuts during
placement. The default `preload=placed` keeps the existing access checks
against the bench and previously placed parts. This hint declares a
preparation operation, not a fixed assembly order.

The generated JSON includes `axis`, `head_position_mm`, `thread_reach_mm`,
and, for a framed nut pocket, `nut_seat_mm`, alongside `position_mm`.
Step renders and driver-clearance checks start at the same head seat.
No model names, coordinates, screw counts or assembly sequences are built
into the planner. Bore and seat frames and hardware specifications are
explicit design metadata; the planner does not infer threads or discover
holes from arbitrary boolean geometry. Bounds checks are not proof that
a bore is empty or that a real tool fits: verify the part geometry too.

Worked examples: the [repaired rail corner](../examples/assemble/corner.assemble)
uses two closed mounting interfaces per outer bracket and six M5 screws;
the [elastic-cord toy bow](../examples/assemble/bow3/bow3.assemble) uses four
M4x12 screws, four captive nuts and recessed head seats. The bow's 8 mm
bearing-seat distance leaves 4 mm of screw past the nut bearing face.

## What it checks

Design errors (reported, and the run exits 1):

- screws that collide inside a host (same spot on one face, or two faces
  meeting at the same station with enough reach to cross);
- too little thread reaching the T-nut (under 3 mm);
- a clamped part that does not sit on the named host face.

Hard predicates on every step: a free part must rest on the bench or on
placed parts and balance on that support (a part about to be screwed may
be held against its host); nothing may reach under the partial assembly
(flip instead); the assembly's centre of mass stays over its bench
contact; a screw's driver cylinder is clear of everything but its own
parts and does not come up through the bench; T-nuts can still enter (drop-in: the slot face is exposed;
slide-in: an end is open and unsealed).

Costs (weights in the `plan` line): flip 10, host rail not lying flat
while its nut is started 6 (also a rail stood on end), driver more than
30° off vertical 3, support polygon shrinking 2, leaving a sibling screw
on the same bracket 1, and 1 per move for each placed part nothing holds
yet.

## Outputs

- `foo_plan.md`: bill of materials, T-nut preload sheet (nuts per rail,
  slot, mm from the marked end), then numbered steps: rest face, parts
  placed, rails preloaded, screws tightened with torque, warnings.
- `foo_plan.json`: the same as data. Each fastener's `position_mm: [x,y,z]`
  is its host-face contact point in the assembled world frame, in millimetres
  (including `inset=` for a recessed peg seat), before the instruction step's
  bench rotation. It is not the screw head position.
- `foo_stepN.scad`: the scene after step N, rest face down, this step's
  parts orange, earlier parts grey, screw heads red, the bench as a grey
  slab just under the rest face. `--png` also renders `foo_stepN.png`
  through OpenSCAD. With OpenSCAD 2025.05 or newer the scenes are fully
  evaluated with the Manifold backend, which costs the same as the preview
  and removes the flicker where mated faces coincide; older OpenSCAD builds
  get the preview and a note (CGAL would be 15 to 25 times slower).

## Companion site

`coscad site DIR a.assemble b.assemble ...` plans each assembly with
renders and writes a static site: `DIR/index.html` (search across builds
by name, part, material, screw spec) and `DIR/<name>/index.html` (step
cards with the render, rest face, parts, preloads, screws with torque,
warnings; prev/next buttons, arrow keys, a filter box, `#step-N` links).
It is plain HTML with the plan JSON embedded, so it works from a file or
any static host (GitHub Pages, a folder on the router box). Light and
dark themes, safe-area padding for phones.

## How the search works

Nodes are part instances, one T-nut batch per rail, and fasteners; edges
are requires-before relations derived from the model (host and clamped
part before a screw, a rail's nuts before its screws and before the parts
clamped to it, slide-in nuts before anything sealing an end). Another part
covering a drop-in slot or a hex pocket is not an edge: `nutCanEnter`
rejects the nuts once the entry is covered, and the order works because
settling loads a host's nuts as soon as it is placed (see "Feature burial"
under Planned). A state
is the placed set plus the rest face. Placing a part settles its
follow-ups (nuts that can now go in, screws whose driver is clear). The
search is a beam over rest-face phases: on one face it greedily takes
every cheap move; it branches on flipping to another face or on accepting
one expensive move. A completion heuristic charges for parts that will
need a flip to reach and for screw groups that cannot be vertical on the
current face.

Search width matters on larger frames. For the 29-part cube fixture the
plan cost was 208 / 183 / 179 / 167 at beam 10 / 30 / 80 / 200, in 3 / 10
/ 25 / 60 seconds; the corner example is instant at any width.

## Limits

- Access and support use bounding boxes, so an L bracket blocks its own
  inner corner and a driver beside a bracket edge may be judged blocked.
- Screw heads and nuts are not solids: contact is between part boxes.
- One torque class per material; the printed default (0.6 Nm) is a guess
  to measure on an offcut.
- Sub-assemblies are not discovered; each recursive `.assemble` is a part
  (see "Sub-assemblies" under Planned).
- Every screw is driven to its final torque in the step it becomes
  reachable; there is no snug-then-torque pass (see "Operations").
- The bench has no size and the finished assembly is not checked against
  a doorway (see "Workspace and delivery").
- Fixtures, clamps, and third hands are not modelled: a warning asks you
  to steady a rail stood on end.
- Internal peg seats and capture order need `inset=` and `after=` hints;
  the planner does not discover them from the solid geometry. The
  [Orbit Tower](https://manual.cswine.cloud/builds/orbit-tower/index.html) is a worked
  example: 20 pieces, 19 peg joints, assembled upright without flips.

## Planned

Not implemented yet. These four extensions come from a separate
prototype planner (`AsmPlan.hs`) that modelled joints, buried features,
staging areas and a doorway on a desk build. The prototype's geometry was
a toy; the ideas below are the parts worth carrying over. Proposed syntax
follows the existing `.assemble` conventions and may change.

### Feature burial as one rule

Today the edges are special cases: clamped part after its nuts, slide-in
nuts before end caps, and a predicate (not an edge) for drop-in slots and
hex pockets. Replace them with one rule:

> Node A must come before part P when A needs access to a region that P
> occupies once placed.

The regions the model already computes are the access regions:

- a drop-in nut's entry box on its slot face (`dropInOk`),
- a hex pocket's entry box (`hexBlocked`, `fPocketEntry`),
- a slide-in nut's end caps (`endCaps`),
- a screw's driver cylinder (`driverBox`): the driver axis is fixed by the
  host face, so a part that would block it later must come after the screw.

For every access region and every part whose oriented box enters it
(other than the node's own host and clamped part), add the edge. The
edges do not depend on the rest face; the bench covering a face stays a
predicate, because a flip clears it. A cycle among these edges is a design
error that names the feature, for example "the top slot of bottom_front
is covered by panel, but panel's screw needs a nut in that slot". This
turns today's dead ends in the search into errors reported before
planning, and the extra edges also prune the search.

### Operations

Steps that join nothing: square a frame, check a diagonal, test a
mechanism before it is enclosed. One line per operation:

```
op square "Check both diagonals; tap the corners square" over=bottom_front,bottom_back,bottom_left,bottom_right before=panel
op check "Spin the rotor by hand; it must turn freely" over=rotor#1 uses=rotor#1.top
```

- `over=` names the parts the operation is about. It runs after every
  fastener that joins two `over=` parts has been started.
- `uses=part.face` names faces that must be exposed. They become access
  regions under the burial rule, so anything that covers them is ordered
  after the operation.
- `before=` and `after=` add explicit edges, as `after=` does on parts.
- The quoted text is printed in the step as written.

Fasteners that join `over=` parts are driven in two passes: snug when
placed ("finger tight, then a quarter turn"), and to their torque in a
"Torque" step after the operation. Assemblies with no `op` lines plan
exactly as now. Operations are nodes in the requires-before graph with no
geometry of their own; they never block a driver or a nut.

### Sub-assemblies

The planner grows one assembly on one bench. Many builds are faster and
steadier as separate pieces joined at the end: two side frames, then the
rails between them.

```
sub left  = upright_fl, upright_bl, bottom_left, top_left, br_front_low_left, br_back_low_left
sub right = upright_fr, upright_br, bottom_right, top_right, br_front_low_right, br_back_low_right
```

- Each `sub` is planned on its own with the fasteners whose clamped part
  and host are both inside it, and gets its own steps ("Build left: 2a,
  2b, ...").
- In the parent plan a finished `sub` is a single rigid part: box is the
  union of its members' boxes, mass is their sum, and it is placed like
  any other part.
- Fasteners that cross a `sub` boundary belong to the parent. Nuts they
  need inside a member are preloaded while that `sub` is built, since a
  member's slot may be covered by the time the parent joint is made; the
  burial rule decides whether that is still possible and reports it if
  not.
- A part may be in at most one `sub`; `sub`s do not nest at first.
  Recursive `.assemble` parts stay as they are.

Discovering sub-assemblies (connected groups that need nothing from the
rest) can come later, once declared ones work.

### Workspace and delivery

Two `plan` options:

```
plan bench=900x600 floor=2000x2000
plan door=690x2040
```

- `bench=WxD` (and optionally `floor=WxD`) sizes the work areas. After
  each step the footprint of the partial assembly in its rest orientation
  must fit an area; the step names the area ("on the bench", "on the
  floor"). Moving from bench to floor costs a flip's weight. A footprint
  larger than every area is a design error. Two sub-assemblies on the
  bench at once must fit side by side.
- `door=WxH` checks the finished assembly's box against an opening in
  each axis-aligned orientation. Tilting through diagonally is not
  considered, so the check is conservative. If the assembly does not
  pass, the planner finds the smallest set of parts it can leave off
  (closed under the requires-before relation: nothing that stays depends
  on them) such that the rest passes, replans with those last, and splits
  the plan into "Before moving" and "After moving it through the door".
  If no such set exists, that is a design error.
